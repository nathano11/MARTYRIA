YES. Exactly. And that might be the simplest way to express what this entire text is doing.  
“The sabbath was made for man, not man for the sabbath.” — Mark 2:27  
That saying establishes a relationship between people and the systems they create. The Sabbath is good because it serves human life. When people are made to suffer in order to preserve the institution, the relationship has been inverted.  
And suddenly, look at the Greek poem again.  
The covenant: Was it made to sustain a community, or does the community exist to sustain the covenant?  
The shepherd: Does the shepherd serve the flock, or does the flock exist to enrich the shepherd?  
The economy: Does wealth serve human needs, or must human needs serve the accumulation of wealth?  
The law: Does the law protect people, or must people be sacrificed to preserve the law?  
The story: Does the story help people understand their lives, or must people distort their lives to fit the story?  
It’s the same inversion, repeated at different scales.  
And it explains why the serpent and the wheel are different. The wheel is the recurring process by which arrangements rise, change, and fall. The serpent is associated with the active reversal in which the arrangement becomes more important than the beings it was supposed to serve.  
The system starts as a means and becomes an end.  
**And that’s why the framing matters more than novelty.**  
You’re right that the underlying ethical principle isn’t new. The biblical tradition itself contains resources for criticizing the elevation of religious observance over human need. Other philosophical and religious traditions have their own ways of questioning the relationship between institutions and the people they govern.  
The interesting thing about this poem is its attempt to make that principle into a unified interpretive lens.  
Once you see the inversion, you start asking a different question about almost everything:  
Not merely Is this institution good or bad?  
But what was this institution supposed to be for, and when did preserving it become more important than fulfilling that purpose?  
That question can change how you understand a law, a religion, a business, a government, a family, or even a revolutionary movement.  
Because a system can begin with a genuinely good purpose and still become destructive when its continued existence becomes the overriding objective.  
And the people defending it may sincerely believe they’re protecting something sacred.  
That’s the unsettling part: the inversion doesn’t require an evil mastermind. It can happen through ordinary people faithfully maintaining an institution whose original purpose they’ve stopped examining.  
**There’s one final twist, though.**  
The poem can itself become subject to the same criticism.  
If the author creates a grand theory explaining how every institution has inverted its proper purpose, readers could start treating that theory as the ultimate authority. They could force every historical example into its framework and dismiss anything that doesn’t fit.  
The lens would become another thing that must be protected at all costs.  
Which would reproduce the very inversion it was designed to expose.  
So perhaps the deepest principle isn’t simply that institutions must serve people.  
It’s that nothing we create to serve human life should become immune from being questioned in the name of human life.  
Not the Sabbath. Not the covenant. Not the kingdom. Not the Jubilee. Not even the story that teaches us to recognize the inversion.  
The poem might not be discovering a new truth.  
It might be making an old truth newly visible by connecting it to a whole network of things we thought we understood separately.  
And sometimes that’s what a powerful idea does: it doesn’t give you another fact to remember. It changes what you notice.
